\section{Architectural Decision: PC-Hosted Deployment via Meta Quest Link}

An early architectural decision concerned the deployment target for the application. Two approaches were considered: a fully standalone build running natively on the Meta Quest 3's Android-based operating system, or a PC-hosted build streamed to the headset via Meta Quest Link.

The standalone approach was initially pursued but presented a fundamental incompatibility: the Logitech racing wheel hardware communicates over a proprietary USB HID protocol that requires host-side driver initialisation. The Quest 3's Android operating system does not support this initialisation sequence, and no accessible mechanism exists to sideload compatible drivers without device rooting, which would void hardware warranties and compromise the Meta Quest Link functionality required for hand tracking pass-through.

The PC-hosted architecture was adopted instead. In this configuration, the Unity application runs natively on a Windows PC. The Meta Quest 3 connects to the PC via USB-C or wirelessly via Air Link, and Meta Quest Link streams the rendered stereoscopic output to the headset while simultaneously relaying head-tracking pose data and hand-tracking skeletal data back to the PC through the OpenXR runtime. The racing wheel, pedal set, and gear shifter connect directly to the PC via USB and are read natively by Unity's Input System.

This architecture offers several practical advantages over the standalone approach:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full hardware compatibility} — the racing wheel initialises normally as it would for any PC game, with no driver limitations.
    \item \textbf{Desktop-class rendering} — the application benefits from PC GPU rendering rather than being constrained to the Quest 3's mobile chipset, permitting higher fidelity vehicle models and environment detail.
    \item \textbf{Retained VR capability} — head tracking and hand tracking function identically to a standalone deployment, since Meta Quest Link passes through the full OpenXR feature set including hand tracking subsystems.
    \item \textbf{Simplified build pipeline} — the Android build target introduces its own category of build issues (Gradle configuration, AAR dependency conflicts, manifest merging) that do not apply to a Windows Standalone build.
\end{itemize}

The trade-off is that a PC must be present during simulator operation, which is acceptable for the intended use case of supervised driving-school training sessions where an instructor workstation is expected to be present regardless.

\section{Hardware Selection and Evaluation}

\subsection{Initial Hardware: Nitho Drive Pro One}
Development began using a Nitho Drive Pro One, a budget-tier consumer racing wheel, to establish the core vehicle control pipeline. User testing during this phase revealed several limitations that motivated a hardware upgrade:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Steering rotation limited to 270°, compared to the 900° rotation range of real vehicle steering.
    \item Undersized wheel diameter relative to an actual vehicle steering wheel, reducing physical immersion.
    \item No separate gear shifter peripheral.
    \item Accelerator and brake pedals positioned close together, causing testers — particularly those without prior VR experience — to misidentify which pedal they were pressing while wearing the headset, since the pedals are not visible during use.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Upgraded Hardware: Logitech G29 and Driving Force Shifter}
Following an application for project funding (Appendix~\ref{app:funding}), a Logitech G29 Driving Force racing wheel and matching Logitech Driving Force Shifter were acquired. The G29 offers 900° rotation, a full-size wheel, force feedback, and substantially greater pedal spacing. All testers who evaluated both units rated the G29 as significantly more realistic. The increased pedal spacing was sufficient to resolve the pedal identification issue observed with the Nitho unit, without requiring a non-standard pedal remapping.

\section{Development Phases}

\subsection{Phase 1: Core Vehicle Physics}
The initial development phase established the vehicle controller using Unity's WheelCollider physics components. A Tata Safari 3D model was rigged with four wheel colliders configured for front-wheel drive. Suspension parameters — spring strength, damper coefficients, and friction curves — were iteratively tuned to approximate the vehicle's real-world ride characteristics. A four-position automatic gearbox (Park, Reverse, Neutral, Drive) was implemented with speed-gated gear-change restrictions to prevent unrealistic gear changes at speed.

\subsection{Phase 2: XR Rig and Hardware Input Integration}
The XR camera rig was anchored to a driver-seat transform inside the vehicle hierarchy so that head-tracked camera movement remained fixed relative to the cockpit rather than the physical room. Hardware input from the racing wheel was integrated through Unity's legacy Input System, with a delayed detection routine to prevent the Quest 3 controllers — which also register as joystick devices when connected via Link — from being mistaken for the racing wheel.

A significant integration challenge arose from the G29's pedal axis behaviour: the pedals report an axis value of $+1$ at rest and $-1$ at full travel, the inverse of the conventional 0-to-1 convention. This was resolved with the remapping function:
\[
\text{input} = \text{Clamp}_{01}\!\left(\frac{-\text{raw}+1}{2}\right)
\]

\subsection{Phase 3: VR Hand Interaction}
VR hand tracking interaction was implemented for the steering wheel and horn using Unity's XR Interaction Toolkit. A passive interaction component (rather than a physics-based grab interactor) was used on the steering wheel mesh to prevent the XR runtime from relocating the mesh transform during grab events — an issue encountered during early implementation where the grab interactable caused the wheel to orbit around the vehicle rather than rotating in place. The corrected implementation records the angular position of the hand relative to the wheel centre at the moment of grab, frozen in the car body's coordinate frame to prevent feedback from the wheel's own rotation, and computes subsequent steering angle from the angular delta.

\subsection{Phase 4: NPC Traffic System}
An initial NPC traffic controller was implemented using per-checkpoint raycasting and a rule-based stop/go system. Testing revealed significant reliability problems: vehicles frequently entered head-on deadlocks at intersections, oversteered on sharp turns causing wheel-spinning-in-place behaviour, and in some cases collided with the player vehicle despite explicit avoidance logic. 

The traffic controller was subsequently redesigned as an explicit finite state machine with five states: \textit{Driving}, \textit{Following}, \textit{StoppedAtLight}, \textit{CrossingIntersection}, and \textit{Yielding}. Key design principles adopted in the redesign include: vehicles never reverse (eliminating a major source of erratic behaviour); the player vehicle is always treated as a yield-priority obstacle rather than an object to steer around; steering uses a single pure-pursuit calculation toward the next checkpoint, hard-clamped to a maximum angle to prevent in-place wheel spinning; and obstacle detection uses a compact three-ray forward sensor fan rather than multiple per-checkpoint raycasts.

\subsection{Phase 5: Environment Construction}
Three training environments were constructed within a single Unity scene to allow instant switching without scene reloading. A straight highway environment provides an obstacle-free space for control familiarisation. A curved road environment introduces sustained steering correction and speed management through a series of sharp bends. A city grid environment — structured as a square perimeter road bisected by one horizontal and one vertical internal road, forming nine intersections — provides signalised intersection navigation with AI traffic. A world-space VR menu, interactable via both hand-ray pointing and hand tracking, allows the player to select and teleport between environments.

\section{Testing Procedures}

\subsection{Informal Iterative Testing}
Throughout development, informal testing sessions were conducted with the supervising faculty member and additional volunteer participants. These sessions focused on identifying usability issues, hardware ergonomics problems, and simulator comfort concerns rather than collecting formalised quantitative metrics, given the project's current stage of development.

\subsection{Planned Formal Evaluation}
Following completion of current refinements — height calibration, NPC traffic tuning, and full hand-tracking integration — a structured evaluation is planned incorporating the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) and System Usability Scale (SUS) administered to a cohort of novice and experienced drivers, alongside quantitative performance logging of frame rate stability and NPC traffic incident frequency.

\section{Summary}
The development methodology for DriveXR followed an iterative approach in which architectural and hardware decisions were revised in response to concrete technical constraints encountered during implementation — most significantly, the shift from a standalone Quest 3 deployment to a PC-hosted Meta Quest Link architecture, and the shift from a budget racing wheel to the Logitech G29. This iterative process, documented in full in this chapter, produced a working simulator validated through informal testing, with formal evaluation identified as near-term future work.
