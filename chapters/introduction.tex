\chapter{Introduction}{\label{ch:intro}}
\section{Background and Motivation}

The integration of Virtual Reality (VR) technologies in driver training has emerged as a promising approach to creating realistic and safe learning environments. Traditional driving instruction faces persistent limitations including safety concerns during hazardous scenario training, prohibitive vehicle costs for diverse fleet exposure, environmental constraints limiting practice opportunities, and scalability challenges for growing driving school enrollment. These limitations are particularly acute when training involves higher-end vehicles, where operational costs, insurance premiums, and maintenance expenses render traditional hands-on training economically impractical for many driving schools and individual learners~\cite{Harari2021}.

Recent advancements in consumer VR hardware, particularly standalone headsets such as the Meta Quest 3, have reached performance and affordability thresholds enabling deployment in educational contexts. Modern VR systems deliver stereoscopic rendering at high refresh rates with low motion-to-photon latency, wide fields of view, and inside-out tracking eliminating external sensor requirements. The Quest 3 additionally offers native optical hand tracking and, critically for this project, support for Meta Quest Link — a feature enabling the headset to operate as a PC-VR display and tracking peripheral while a Unity application runs natively on a connected Windows PC.

This convergence of technologies creates an opportunity to develop immersive driving simulations that closely replicate real-world vehicle dynamics, visual fidelity, and driver control feedback using consumer-accessible hardware. This project presents \textbf{DriveXR}, a VR-based driving simulator combining a Meta Quest 3 headset with a Logitech G29 racing wheel and pedal set, running as a PC-hosted application via Meta Quest Link. The system is intended to help novice drivers safely practise fundamental vehicle control skills before driving on public roads.

\section{Problem Statement}

Despite growing interest in VR driving simulation, current systems exhibit limitations that hinder educational adoption:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{High Cost Barriers:} Commercial driving simulators used in some professional training contexts often cost tens of thousands of dollars per unit, placing them beyond the financial reach of most driving schools and educational institutions.
    
    \item \textbf{Platform-Hardware Incompatibility:} Physically realistic racing wheel hardware communicates over USB HID protocols requiring host-side driver support. This creates a direct incompatibility with fully standalone VR headset deployment, a constraint not always addressed in existing lightweight VR driving research platforms.
    
    \item \textbf{Entertainment vs Education Focus:} Commercially available racing simulator software targets entertainment use cases rather than structured driver education, generally lacking progressive-difficulty training environments or NPC traffic behaviour suited to intersection navigation practice.
    
    \item \textbf{Limited Hardware Fidelity Evaluation:} Few accessible projects document the practical usability trade-offs between budget and premium physical control hardware specifically in the context of VR-headset-worn use, where users cannot visually reference the pedals or controls they are operating.
    
    \item \textbf{Limited Validation:} Few studies demonstrate empirical transfer of VR training to real-world driving performance, creating uncertainty about pedagogical effectiveness for driving schools considering adoption.
\end{enumerate}

There exists a clear opportunity for an accessible, consumer-hardware-based VR driving simulator that resolves the platform-hardware compatibility constraint, supports structured progressive-difficulty training environments, and documents the practical engineering and usability trade-offs involved — informing both this project's own development and future similar systems.

\section{Research Objectives}

The primary objective of this project is to design, implement, and preliminarily evaluate a VR driving simulator built from consumer-accessible hardware, providing an immersive and realistic training experience suitable for novice driver skill development. The specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{System Architecture Design:} Develop a VR simulation architecture integrating a Meta Quest 3 headset, Unity 6 game engine, and commercial racing wheel hardware, resolving the platform compatibility constraints identified during development.
    
    \item \textbf{Realistic Vehicle Simulation:} Implement physics-based vehicle dynamics for a Tata Safari model, tuned to approximate real-world handling characteristics using Unity's WheelCollider physics system.
    
    \item \textbf{Physical Control Integration:} Integrate a Logitech G29 racing wheel and pedal set with correct axis mapping and ergonomic pedal configuration, informed by direct comparison against a budget alternative.
    
    \item \textbf{VR Hand Interaction:} Implement hand-tracking-based interaction for in-cockpit controls including the steering wheel and horn, alongside physical hardware input.
    
    \item \textbf{Training Environment Development:} Build a progressive-difficulty set of training environments — straight highway, curved road, and signalised city grid — with an adaptive NPC traffic system for the city environment.
    
    \item \textbf{Preliminary Usability Evaluation:} Conduct informal testing sessions to identify key usability factors including simulator sickness patterns, hardware ergonomics, and VR headset calibration requirements, informing a future formal user study.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}

\subsection{In Scope}
The system development encompasses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A PC-hosted VR application streamed to the Meta Quest 3 via Meta Quest Link.
    \item Simulation of a single vehicle model (Tata Safari) with tuned WheelCollider-based physics.
    \item Three training environments of progressive difficulty: straight highway, curved road, and signalised city grid with NPC traffic.
    \item Automatic transmission simulation (Park, Reverse, Neutral, Drive).
    \item Physical racing wheel and pedal input via Logitech G29, with gear shifter support via Logitech Driving Force Shifter.
    \item VR hand-tracking interaction for the steering wheel and horn.
    \item Informal preliminary testing with the supervising faculty member and additional volunteer participants.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Out of Scope}
The following are explicitly excluded from the current project phase:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Force feedback steering resistance (hardware-capable but not yet integrated in software).
    \item Manual transmission / clutch-engagement simulation.
    \item Motion platform or vestibular simulation.
    \item Multi-user networked scenarios.
    \item Automated performance scoring and assessment logging.
    \item Formal regulatory certification or driving-school pilot deployment.
    \item Formal structured user study with standardised instruments (SSQ, SUS) — identified as near-term future work.
\end{itemize}
