\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}
This project set out to address well-documented limitations in traditional driver training: cost, safety risk, and limited access to higher-end vehicles during instruction. Through literature review and iterative development, DriveXR was designed and implemented as a VR-based driving simulator combining a consumer-grade Meta Quest 3 headset with a Logitech G29 racing wheel, running as a PC-hosted application via Meta Quest Link.

The system requirements analysis (Chapter~\ref{ch:system_analysis}) established the functional and non-functional requirements grounded in the reviewed literature. A key architectural decision made during development — and one that diverged from the originally proposed standalone-with-custom-hardware approach — was the adoption of a PC-hosted deployment model using commodity racing hardware rather than custom Bluetooth-enabled controls. This decision was driven by a concrete technical constraint: the Quest 3's Android operating system does not support the driver initialisation sequence required by USB HID racing wheels, making a standalone deployment with physical wheel hardware infeasible without device modification. The PC-hosted architecture, detailed in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, resolved this constraint while preserving full head-tracking and hand-tracking functionality via Meta Quest Link's OpenXR pass-through.

Similarly, the originally proposed custom ESP32-based Bluetooth steering and pedal hardware was superseded by the Logitech G29, a commercially available racing wheel offering 900° rotation and force feedback, following an evaluation that found a budget consumer wheel (Nitho Drive Pro One) insufficiently realistic for the project's aims. This substitution reduced development risk associated with custom hardware fabrication while still meeting the project's core requirement of physically realistic control input, as confirmed through user testing (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).

\section{Contributions}
This work makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A working PC-hosted VR driving simulator} combining consumer VR hardware (Meta Quest 3) with commodity racing peripherals (Logitech G29) via a Meta Quest Link streaming architecture, achieving desktop-class rendering quality without requiring dedicated simulation hardware or custom electronics.
    
    \item \textbf{A three-environment progressive difficulty training framework} — straight highway, curved road, and signalised city grid — implemented within a single Unity scene with instant environment switching via a VR-interactable world-space menu.
    
    \item \textbf{An adaptive NPC traffic controller} redesigned as an explicit finite state machine incorporating obstacle avoidance, traffic signal compliance, intersection yield-priority resolution, and player-vehicle right-of-way, developed iteratively in response to reliability problems identified in an earlier raycast-based implementation.
    
    \item \textbf{Empirical hardware evaluation} comparing budget and premium racing wheel hardware, documenting the specific ergonomic factors — pedal spacing, wheel rotation range, wheel diameter — that affect usability for VR-headset-wearing users who cannot visually reference physical controls during use.
    
    \item \textbf{Preliminary usability findings} on VR-specific challenges in driving simulation, including simulator sickness adaptation patterns across repeated sessions and headset height/positional calibration requirements arising from Quest 3 room-scale tracking in a fixed-seat context.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}

Several limitations of the current system are acknowledged:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Validation is preliminary.} Testing to date has been informal, involving the supervising faculty member and a small number of additional participants. A structured user study using standardised instruments (SSQ, SUS) with a larger and more diverse participant cohort, as outlined in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, has not yet been conducted.
    
    \item \textbf{NPC traffic reliability at high density.} While substantially improved through the state-machine redesign, intersection deadlocks and turning difficulties at sharp intersection geometries were not fully eliminated, particularly under high simulated traffic density (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).
    
    \item \textbf{PC dependency.} Unlike a fully standalone deployment, the current architecture requires a connected PC, which — while acceptable for the supervised training context this project targets — limits portability compared to a self-contained headset-only solution.
    
    \item \textbf{No force feedback.} The G29 supports force feedback steering resistance, but this feature has not yet been integrated into the simulation, meaning road-surface and steering-load feedback are not currently simulated.
    
    \item \textbf{Height calibration is manual.} The current system does not provide an in-VR mechanism for users to adjust seated eye height, requiring scene-level configuration rather than a runtime calibration step.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}

\subsection{Near-Term Improvements}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Seated-mode height calibration.} Implement a runtime calibration step allowing users to set their eye-level offset within the cockpit independent of the Quest 3's room-scale floor reference, directly addressing the height-related usability issue identified in testing.
    
    \item \textbf{NPC traffic refinement.} Widen intersection turning geometry and reduce simulated vehicle spawn density at high-traffic intersections to further reduce deadlock frequency, alongside continued tuning of the state-machine yield-priority thresholds.
    
    \item \textbf{Completion of hand-tracking integration.} Extend current hand-tracking interactions (steering wheel, horn) to cover the full control surface including gear shifter interaction and in-VR menu navigation without reliance on ray-pointing alone.
    
    \item \textbf{Formal user study.} Conduct the structured evaluation protocol outlined in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, incorporating SSQ and SUS instruments across a larger participant cohort spanning novice and experienced drivers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Medium-Term Extensions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Force feedback integration.} Implement steering resistance and road-surface feedback using the G29's native force feedback motor, informed by prior research linking haptic fidelity to training transfer effectiveness.
    
    \item \textbf{Additional training scenarios.} Extend the environment library to include adverse weather conditions, night driving, and emergency response scenarios such as sudden obstacle avoidance.
    
    \item \textbf{Performance assessment module.} Introduce structured, logged performance metrics — speed compliance, lane discipline, signal compliance, following distance — to support objective learner assessment, an area intentionally deferred during the current development phase to prioritise core simulation stability.
    
    \item \textbf{Standalone deployment investigation.} Explore alternative approaches to standalone Quest 3 deployment with physical hardware input, such as a PC-bridge companion application relaying wheel input to the headset over a local network connection, to reduce PC dependency while retaining hardware realism.
\end{itemize}

\section{Final Remarks}
DriveXR demonstrates that a credible VR driving training platform can be built from consumer-grade hardware — a Meta Quest 3 headset and a commercially available racing wheel — without requiring custom electronics or dedicated simulation infrastructure. The development process surfaced concrete engineering challenges not fully anticipated at the project's outset, most significantly the platform incompatibility between USB racing wheel hardware and standalone Quest deployment, and the behavioural instability of early rule-based NPC traffic logic. Resolving these challenges — through the PC-hosted Meta Quest Link architecture and the state-machine traffic controller redesign respectively — constituted a substantial portion of the practical engineering contribution of this work.

The system in its current state provides a functional, testable foundation for VR-based driver training. The identified limitations, particularly around formal validation and NPC traffic behaviour at high density, define a clear and achievable roadmap for continued development toward a system suitable for structured evaluation in an educational deployment context.
